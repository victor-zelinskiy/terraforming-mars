```
You are illustrating the CORPORATION card "Lotus Innovations" for a fan expansion of the board game Terraforming Mars. Attached: the card scan — copy the corporate logo from its top-left corner. Generate ONE finished illustration right away, without asking questions.

WHO THEY ARE. Lotus Innovations started with one college graduate who invented a new oxygen recirculation system for his thesis and brought it to market. Many years and patents later, the company stands on the cutting edge of terraforming technology: its atmospheric processors make the Martian air thicker every year, and every gain it makes for the planet comes back to it as data, measurements and new patents. Lotus is still a research company at heart — young engineers, open-plan labs, test rigs running day and night — and it is proud of where it came from.

THE PICTURE. A wide view inside the main test hall of Lotus Innovations' research centre on Mars, on an ordinary working day. The hall is bright and clean: white composite wall panels, a light grey floor with painted test-bay markings, a mezzanine walkway along one side, plain white LED ceiling panels. In the centre of the frame, on a steel test stand, stands a full-scale prototype of a new atmospheric processor module — a tall stack of heat exchangers, filters, compressors and ducting, its side panels open, sensors and cables taped to it, a few panels still missing. Engineers work around it: a woman in a light lab coat reading values off a rugged laptop on a cart, two technicians fitting a duct flange, a very young intern carrying a box of labelled data drives towards a rack of sample archives and storage servers along the wall — the measurements are what the company lives on. Along one side, a tall window band looks out onto the Martian plain under a pale butterscotch sky, where a line of full-size production atmospheric processors stands in the distance, each releasing a thin pale plume — the work this hall exists for. Inside, near the window, a row of planters with real green plants. Only one detail tells the founder's story, to be found on the second look: on a cluttered engineer's desk in the foreground, a small, crude, home-made oxygen recirculation unit — the founder's original thesis prototype, battered and held together with tape — is still running, keeping a single green seedling alive under a glass bell jar.

THE LOGO — the centre of the image. Mounted on the far wall of the hall above the test stand, in the exact centre of the frame, about one quarter of the image width: the Lotus Innovations logo copied faithfully from the scan — a stylised lotus flower of three purple petals (a tall central petal and two side petals curving outward) with white outlines, above two dark-green leaf shapes curving out to the left and right at the base; to its right the word LOTUS in heavy italic purple capitals and beneath it the word INNOVATIONS in heavy italic dark-green capitals (letters spelled exactly L-O-T-U-S and I-N-N-O-V-A-T-I-O-N-S). Built as flat painted panels fixed to the wall, lit by the same ceiling light as the rest of the hall — no glow, no backlight, no neon. The lotus symbol may repeat very small on a lab coat or on the side of the prototype. No other text, no other logo.

STYLE — match the original Terraforming Mars cards, which are photographs of real places: a photograph or sober engineering visualisation of a real research facility, NOT cinematic concept art; think of a documentary photograph of a real engineering test hall at a technology company; standard lens 35–50 mm from standing eye height near the foreground desk, slightly off-axis so the hall and the test bays run diagonally while the logo stays centred; light only from plausible sources — neutral white ceiling panels and the daylight from the window band, soft shadows; NO volumetric god-rays, glowing haze, lens flares, mirror-polished floors or bloom; a light, clean palette of white, pale grey and steel, with the purple and green of the logo repeated only in a few small places (a cable tag, a mug, a stripe on a cart), the green of the plants and the rust and butterscotch of Mars beyond the window; no teal-and-orange grade, no sterile sci-fi blue tint; hardware real and USED (cable runs, tape, fasteners, scuffs on the floor, labels without readable text, a coffee cup, a jacket on a chair); modest human scale — a lab, not a cathedral; slight grain, no gloss, no AI sheen.
```
